The Marr–Albus idea (1969, 1971)

1

Expand

Spread a few inputs over very many granule cells, only a few active at once. States that looked alike become easy to tell apart.

2

Read out

A Purkinje cell adds up the active granule cells, each through its own synaptic weight.

3

Correct

When the climbing fibre reports an error, change only the synapses that were active just then.

All the learning lives in one place: the granule-to-Purkinje synapses.

The cerebellum in a walking robot · 5

David Marr (1969) and James Albus (1971) proposed this independently: the cerebellum as a learning machine, a perceptron with a huge random expansion in front. Everything else stays fixed and random; that is why the brain in this course is called untrained: nothing is trained beforehand, it learns while it lives.
